Why Dating Apps Break

Catfishing isn’t a user problem it’s a system problem. My team diagnosed the whole failure chain on Tinder, traced it to one root cause, and designed a system to fix it.

The Problem

“Meet Filbert, 30.”

[IMAGE: “Filbert, 30” catfish Tinder card opener]

We opened our presentation with a joke that isn’t really a joke: a literal catfish with a Tinder profile. Then the real number our research found roughly 79% of users reported interacting with a catfish.

The question we set out to answer wasn’t “how do we catch catfish.” It was: why does the system make catfishing so easy? That reframe drove the entire project.

Process

A weakness at every stage

[IMAGE: Stage breakdown research slides]

We mapped the problem against each stage of a user’s journey on the platform and found a failure at each one.

Sign-Up

Account creation takes under a minute. Users can create multiple accounts with new emails and phone numbers with no friction.

Verification

Verification is always optional, with a 24-72 hour wait. 51.6% of users deem it unnecessary fewer than 20% are actually verified.

Matching

62% of users say matches feel random or low-quality. 41.3% were frustrated by mismatched outcomes.

Recency

22.5% of users hadn’t updated their photos in over six months meaning profiles don’t reflect who people actually are.

Moderation

1 moderator per ~50,000 users. 87% of reports are decided in under two minutes. 89% of banned users return via new credentials. 60% of reporters give up.

[IMAGE: Root-cause slide]

Every stage pointed to the same thing: Tinder’s business model rewards volume, not trust. Faster sign-ups and more users mean more revenue, so the friction that would improve safety works directly against the product’s own metrics.

That move from scattered stage-level failures to one underlying business incentive was the core finding of the project.

The Solution

A system that re-prices trust

[IMAGE: Future State / verification-tier slides]

Because the problem was systemic, so was the fix. We proposed a future-state system that makes trust visible and rewarded at every stage:

  • 01 Account creation: require 2+ identity sources; flag old or edited photos via metadata; new accounts earn visibility through profile quality, not by default.
  • 02 Verification: mandatory facial scan + ID, cross-referenced against public databases. Visible trust tiers: no checkmark (unverified), green (1 ID), blue (fully approved).
  • 03 Recency: photo-refresh policy, engagement incentives, and activity audits to keep profiles current.
  • 04 Moderation: regional moderation weighted by severity; device and data identifiers to block returning banned users; case-tracking notifications so reporters don’t abandon complaints.

The Stakes

Moderation failure isn’t abstract

[IMAGE: Anonymized case-example slide]

We grounded the moderation failures in a documented real-world case reported by [outlet]: a man with multiple sexual-assault allegations who remained active on the platform and was algorithmically boosted into a premium visibility section long after being reported.

This isn’t an edge case. It’s what the current incentive structure produces at scale.

Outcome

Beyond Tinder

[IMAGE: “Beyond Tinder” thesis slide]

The volume-first failure isn’t unique to dating apps the same dynamic shows up in vacation rentals, job platforms, and social networks wherever scale is optimized over trust.

Our broader thesis: private, verified communities trade raw volume for trust, and that trade is where these categories are heading. We presented the project to industry executives and received a grade A.

Reflection

What I took from it

As team lead, my job was to keep eight people aimed at the same question instead of chasing eight different answers. The hardest discipline was resisting the obvious fix a UI patch and making the team follow the evidence through each stage until it led somewhere more honest: a business-incentive root cause. That’s the part I’m most proud of.
