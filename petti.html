What Petti Is

A Tamagotchi for your screen time

[IMAGE: Hero shot key screens + custom Sewer artwork]

I use a screen time blocker called Opal every day. The problem: I was still on my phone five-plus hours a day and I knew I needed to stop. Existing blockers tell you to stop but they don’t give you a reason to care.

After a conversation with a friend who loved Tamagotchi, I had the idea: what if your phone time directly affected a digital pet? Not a productivity streak an actual character you’d feel bad hurting. Petti was born from that.

The Mascot

“I rescued a blind stray cat and everyone who met her fell in love.”

[IMAGE: Sewer sprite sheet / original character art]

During my junior year of college I rescued a stray cat. She was blind. Her name is Sewer. Everyone who met her immediately loved her and that gave me the mechanic. If Sewer is the mascot, users will hesitate to open apps knowing it means hurting a digital version of her.

That emotional attachment is the product. Sewer isn’t decoration on top of a blocker app she is the reason the blocker works.

Designing the Visual System

A full custom UI built from scratch

[IMAGE: Key app screens showing the UI system across states]

Every visual in Petti is original designed in Figma and Adobe Illustrator, not pulled from a library. The goal was a visual system where the art and the interface feel like one thing, not a character dropped onto a generic UI.

  • Deterioration Sewer’s state visibly degrades the more time you spend on blocked apps a direct, felt consequence rather than a number on a dashboard.
  • Care system Food, attention, and care mechanics keep users returning to Petti instead of their other apps.
  • Love bar A persistent indicator of Sewer’s wellbeing the emotional hook that makes the stakes feel real.
  • Backstory Sewer has an origin story. Context makes her feel like a character you adopted, not a game mechanic you unlocked.

Shipping It

From Figma to the App Store

[IMAGE: Figma frames beside the live app]

I designed every screen in Figma and Illustrator, then used Claude to convert those designs into production application code. Watching Sewer actually move on screen for the first time that moment made the whole process worth it.

While the app waits for App Store approval, I’m already thinking about what’s next: an item shop, expanded world-building features, and mechanics that make Sewer’s world feel even more alive.

Designing for a Younger Audience

Responsibility was a design constraint, not an afterthought

Petti’s audience starts around age 10, which meant privacy and safety had to be built in from the start not patched in later. I was not willing to ship something that took advantage of the people it was supposed to help.

Concretely: I added constraints on sign-ups, limited data collection to only what the app needs to function, and kept the entire experience age-friendly. The visual tone cute, character-driven, non-threatening wasn’t just a style choice; it was appropriate for the audience.

Outcome

Waiting on Apple

[IMAGE: App Store submission screenshot or live product screens]

Petti is currently in Apple App Store review. The product is complete and submitted the next step is shipping it to real users and watching how they respond to Sewer.

Reflection

What I took from it

You don’t have to reinvent the wheel. Screen time blockers already exist. Tamagotchi already exists. What didn’t exist was the specific combination a blocker that uses emotional attachment instead of willpower as its mechanism. Improving something you know is already flawed is sometimes the most honest thing you can do for users.
